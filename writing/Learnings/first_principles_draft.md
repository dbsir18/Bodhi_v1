# Why Founders Love First Principles Thinking

There is a version of the SpaceX origin story that has been told so many times it barely needs Elon Musk's name attached anymore. A founder goes shopping for a rocket. The quotes come back around sixty million dollars. Instead of paying it, he asks what a rocket is actually made of, aerospace-grade aluminum, titanium, copper, carbon fiber, prices out the raw materials on the commodity market, and finds they cost about two percent of what anyone was charging him. He builds the rocket himself. SpaceX is born.

Search for that story and you will find it retold, almost word for word, in blog posts, LinkedIn threads, pitch-deck decks and founder newsletters that have nothing to do with aerospace. It gets cited by fintech founders, edtech founders, D2C founders, none of whom have ever priced a kilogram of titanium in their life. The story has been separated from rockets entirely. What survives is the shape of it: someone refused to accept the market price as information, went underneath it, and found the truth was cheaper than the rumor.

That shape has a name now. First principles thinking. And the interesting question isn't whether it works. Plenty of good reasoning has come out of people asking what something is actually made of instead of what it costs to buy. The interesting question is why founders specifically, more than engineers, more than scientists, more than the philosophers who apparently invented the term, have turned one physics anecdote into the most repeated line in their entire professional vocabulary.

I've been on both sides of this phrase, which is why I feel qualified to poke at it. I've been the person who received it as advice when I was lost, and I've been the person across the table deciding whether it means anything when someone says it to me. It does, and it doesn't, and the difference is worth writing down.

## The problem every founder actually has

Start with what a founder's job structurally requires. Somewhere in the first six months, almost every founder has to make a decision that every expert in the room, every advisor, every person with more experience than them, thinks is wrong. That is not an edge case. It is close to the definition of the job. If deferring to accumulated expertise reliably produced the right call, incumbents would keep winning and nobody would need to start anything new.

So founders have a permanent, structural need for a way to justify ignoring people who know more than they do. "I don't know the rules of this industry" is not something you can say out loud to an investor. "I looked past the rules of this industry to something more fundamental" is the same sentence, but it converts ignorance into a method. First principles thinking is, among other things, the single most efficient piece of language available for making inexperience sound like rigor.

This is not cynicism about any individual founder's honesty. It's a description of what the phrase is structurally suited to do. It takes the most vulnerable moment in a founder's story, the point where they had less information than the people telling them to stop, and turns it into the point where they had more.

## The story gets written after the decision, not before

Here is the part almost nobody sitting inside a first-principles anecdote wants to examine too closely: real decisions under real uncertainty rarely happen as a clean chain of deductions. They happen as an intuition, a bet, a half-formed hunch that gets tested against the world, revised, occasionally salvaged by luck. Musk's own account of costing out a rocket down to its raw materials might be entirely accurate as a description of one afternoon of his actual thinking. But as a template that thousands of founders now reach for to describe their own decisions, it does something else. It offers a retroactive shape.

I know this from the inside, because I have my own version of that story. There was a project, a critical one, for an intelligence agency, and I was the entire team. No engineers, no product managers, nobody staffed under me. Just me, a client whose requirements I had to understand, a solution I had to build, and a deployment I had to run. All three were things I had never done before. I was filled with anxiety, the kind where you don't even know which question to ask first. I remember staring at a blank screen for hours, convinced that everyone who had ever trusted me with this had made a terrible mistake. The best piece of advice I got had nothing to do with the tech stack or the timeline. It was: go back to the root cause, think first principles. So I went back to the drawing board, asked what the problem was actually made of, which constraints were real and which were inherited, and built the solution up from the ground. It worked. The project shipped. The deployment ran.

I tell that story now as if it were a clean chain of deductions. It wasn't. The advice arrived as permission before it arrived as method, and the reasoning came after the permission. Psychologists have a name for the general version of this: the narrative fallacy, the tendency to impose a coherent, causal story on a sequence of events that was actually shaped by contingency and noise. Founders are unusually exposed to this pressure because they are constantly being asked, by investors, by journalists, by their own future employees, to explain why they did what they did. "I got lucky, guessed right, and it happened to work" is a true answer that satisfies nobody, including the founder telling it to themselves at three in the morning. "I reasoned from first principles" is a false sense of inevitability wrapped around the exact same set of facts, and it is a much better answer to have to live with.

That is not a knock on any particular founder's memory. It's closer to a description of what memory does to anyone who has to keep telling a high-stakes story about their own judgment. The uncertainty gets edited out on the way to the retelling, and what's left looks a lot more like physics than it did at the time. I can't always tell the difference between someone who reasoned live and someone who's retelling. I've been both, and the difference isn't visible from the outside.

## Borrowed credibility

There's a second, quieter thing happening whenever a founder invokes first principles, and it has nothing to do with reasoning at all. It's a citation. Musk traces the term to a physics PhD he abandoned at Stanford. Peter Thiel ties it to philosophy in Zero to One. The lineage people reach for runs through Aristotle, sometimes through Feynman, always through someone whose authority came from a domain where truth is unusually settled and checkable.

Startups are the opposite of that. Almost nothing about whether a given company will work is settled or checkable in advance. A founder operates entirely inside guesses, market timing, taste, other people's willingness to say yes. Reaching for a term with a two-thousand-year lineage in physics and philosophy is a way of importing some of that domain's certainty into a domain that structurally has none. It doesn't make the startup's underlying uncertainty go away. It makes the founder's account of their own decision sound like it belongs to a field where uncertainty has already been handled by someone else, a long time ago, and settled in Aristotle's favor.

## Why founders want to hire the same reflex

The instinct doesn't stay contained to how founders talk about themselves. It shows up just as reliably in who they hire, and for reasons that have less to do with the employee's actual cognition than with the founder's own workload.

A founder who has to personally re-litigate every unconventional decision with every new hire runs out of hours fast. What actually scales isn't process, because at the earliest stage there usually isn't enough process yet to hand anyone. What scales is a team that tends to land somewhere close to the founder's own kind of conclusion without being walked there each time. "We want first principles thinkers" is, underneath the flattering language in the job post, a request for people who won't need the founder in the room to greenlight a departure from standard practice. It's an attempt to clone judgment at a stage where there's nothing else to clone.

There's a second function here, less generous but just as real. An early employee who leans on experience, who says "I've seen this fail before at my last company," is doing something a founder often needs blocked rather than heard, because the entire bet being placed is that this time is different. Screening for first-principles thinkers in a job post is also, structurally, screening for people less likely to raise the objection that pattern-matches the plan to somebody else's failure. That objection is sometimes exactly right. A team selected for distrusting precedent is, by construction, a team less likely to be the one that says so.

And the phrase does real work in recruiting itself, separate from anyone's actual thinking once hired. "First principles thinker" in a job description functions as a filter before it functions as a job description. It tells a candidate, correctly, that there won't be a playbook, that ambiguity is the default operating condition, and that whoever takes the role will be expected to build the process rather than inherit one. People who find that appealing self-select in. People who want structure self-select out, usually before the interview happens. The phrase is doing work that has almost nothing to do with epistemology and almost everything to do with risk tolerance.

## The pitch it's actually good for

None of this would matter much if the phrase only lived inside founders' heads. It doesn't. It lives inside pitch meetings, where it does a specific, mechanical job. Investors hear hundreds of ideas that are structurally the same idea with the branding changed. The fastest way to sound like the exception is to claim you didn't get here by looking at competitors at all. "We noticed everyone charges X and decided to do the same thing, slightly cheaper" is an honest description of a huge number of successful companies. It is also a sentence that has never once appeared in a pitch deck. "We went back to what the customer actually needs and rebuilt the category from there" is the same company, in the same market, with a completely different relationship to the person deciding whether to write a check.

This is where the phrase stops being about cognition at all and becomes almost entirely about differentiation. A founder doesn't need to have privately reasoned through anything in the room. They need the room to believe the founder in front of them is running on a different operating system than the ten other people who pitched that week. First principles thinking is the shortest sentence available that makes an ordinary bet sound like an extraordinary insight.

## From the evaluator's seat

I've sat where the people evaluating those pitches sit, and I've been on the hiring side of the same filter. There's a question that ends up being the real filter, whether anyone says it out loud or not: is this the best person to build this? That question reduces to two things, and they're not the ones most people think. The first is: how deeply has this person thought about this? The second is: how much does this person know about the industry or sector?

The second one is easy. You can always read and talk to people. You can acquire industry knowledge in months. It's real, it matters, but it's not the thing that separates anyone.

The first one is the thing. And it can't be acquired by reading. It can only be demonstrated by reasoning from the ground up, by someone who can tell you how and why this will actually work, not just that they believe it will. That's what first principles thinking actually is, from the evaluator's seat: not a philosophy, but a proof of work.

When I'm on the hiring side, the question shifts to a version of the same filter: can this person solve unsolved problems, and in how many ways? The first-principles thinker in a job post tells a candidate, correctly, that there won't be a playbook, that ambiguity is the default operating condition, and that whoever takes the role will be expected to build the process rather than inherit one. People who find that appealing self-select in. People who want structure self-select out, usually before the interview happens. The phrase is doing work that has almost nothing to do with epistemology and almost everything to do with risk tolerance.

## What doing it for real actually looks like

None of that means the underlying skill is fake, only that the phrase and the skill have become two different things, and the second is worth separating out from the first. If you're the employee this whole essay has been about, the one being screened for the reflex in an interview, there are a few concrete moves that produce the real version instead of the retold one.

Separate physics from policy. For any constraint you're handed, whether it's a price, a deadline, or a "that's just how we do it," ask whether it's fixed because reality enforces it or because a habit, a vendor contract, or somebody's old decision does. Musk's actual move with the rocket wasn't cleverness. It was refusing to accept that a quoted price described the materials rather than the market. The same question works on a SaaS renewal, a hiring timeline, a design pattern nobody remembers choosing.

Trace the number back to its parts. When you're told something costs what it costs or takes as long as it takes, break the total into what it's actually made of. Most quoted numbers are markup and inertia sitting on top of a much smaller irreducible cost. Finding the irreducible part is the entire method. Everything else is commentary.

Write the assumption down before you accept it. Before adopting a practice because it's what your last company did, put the assumption underneath it into one sentence, and check whether that sentence is still true in this specific case. Most inherited practices survive this test badly. They usually weren't wrong when they were built. They were built for a constraint that no longer applies.

And write your reasoning down before you know how it turns out, not after. This is the one move that actually defends against everything the rest of this essay describes. In my own project, the version of me that was anxious and lost didn't write anything down. The version of me that rebuilt did. Assumptions, one sentence each, checked against reality. Written before I knew whether any of it was right. The difference between those two versions of me wasn't intelligence. It was that the second one had a record.

A note with a timestamp, written before the result, is the only thing that can later prove the reasoning was there in the first place, rather than added afterward to make a lucky guess look like a method. The narrative fallacy only has raw material to work with once the outcome is known and the story gets built backward from it. A record breaks that.

It's a small habit. It's also the entire difference between actually thinking this way and just being good at describing it once it worked. I still tell the story about that project. The difference is I now know it's a story, and I keep the receipts that make it true. The phrase gave me permission when I needed it most. The practice is what made the permission worth having.
