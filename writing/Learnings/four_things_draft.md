# The Four Things That Actually Matter

There's a question every VC ends up asking a founder in some form or another, why are you the best person to build this, and I've noticed the answers that actually land are never the strategic ones, never the market size slide or the unfair advantage bullet point. They're personal, almost uncomfortably so, some old scar or obsession that made a particular problem impossible to walk past. Nobody ever asks the same question about how you're spending the rest of your life, the actual whole of it, but I've been thinking lately that the honest answer would be just as personal, and probably more useful to sit with than most of what I read about productivity or success.

For me it keeps coming back to four things, and I want to be upfront that I don't think of them as a checklist you tick off in some tidy order. Income, and ideally from something you actually enjoy doing, not just tolerate for the paycheck. Health, not the version that's about how you look in a photo but the version that's about whether your body actually lets you do the things you want to do with your one life. A close circle, friends and family both, the kind you'd genuinely choose again if you had to. And a partner, which if I'm honest is probably the single heaviest decision most of us will ever make, and somehow the one we spend the least deliberate thought on compared to how much thought we put into, say, a career move. I don't have this figured out. I'm writing this as much to work it out for myself as to say anything useful to whoever's reading it.

## Income, ideally from something you enjoy

Back at BITS, if you asked most of us what we actually wanted after graduation, underneath whatever polished answer came out, it was some version of how much and how fast. I lost count of how many times I heard a version of I'd work sixteen hours a day for whoever pays the most, and I don't think anyone saying it was lying or being crass about it. I think most of us just hadn't run the actual experiment yet, the one that eventually tells you the truth about that plan whether you're ready to hear it or not.

The experiment runs itself, honestly, if you just keep living long enough. You get the raise. Income doubles, and there's a strange quiet afterward where you notice how little actually shifted underneath it. The apartment's nicer, the flights are more comfortable, and you're still somehow the same person carrying the same low hum of Sunday-night dread about Monday, just slightly better dressed for it. Everyone tells you this. Almost nobody believes it until they've lived it. Once you have, though, you start looking for something else entirely, and for me that turned out to be satisfaction, in the plainest sense, some proof that the work I did actually mattered to a real person on the other end of it. Made them faster. Or more at peace. Or just a little less overwhelmed than they were before I showed up.

I think about one of my closest friends from university a lot in this context. He spent five years getting seriously, almost obsessively fit, and now runs a fitness coaching business. If you only knew him from Instagram you'd assume his life is the fantasy version of a life, gym in the morning, good coffee, long unbothered afternoons in Bali. I actually lived with him for two weeks not too long ago, and what surprised me wasn't that it was work, I'd assumed that much, it was how much of every single day was work, quietly, behind the parts that make it onto the feed. When I asked him why he still loved it this much after five years, the answer wasn't the income, though he'd be the first to say that matters too. It was watching someone hit a goal they'd set for themselves six months earlier, or twelve, or eighteen, and knowing he'd had a real hand in getting them there. That's the actual thing he's building. The Bali photos are just what it looks like from the outside.

I don't think there's a shortcut to finding whatever your version of that is. The grass genuinely does look greener somewhere else for longer than feels comfortable. There's a real stretch where you're not sure what you're actually good at versus what simply pays well right now, and for years those two things can look identical from the inside. What I've noticed, watching people including myself get somewhere close to an answer, is that there seem to be two different roads that both arrive at the same place. Some people learn to love the daily texture of the work itself, the process of it. Others build something first, almost blindly, and only come to love it in hindsight once they can actually see the shape of what it turned into. I don't think one route is more honest than the other. They just look different on the way there.

## Health

I was an extremely skinny kid, genuinely bad at anything physical, the kind of kid whose shirts always hung a size too loose no matter what I bought. A short trek could put me somewhere close to real distress, and I'm not exaggerating that for effect. Like most people's fitness stories, mine started as vanity, wanting to look a certain way, nothing deeper than that at the start. But I've come to think that's just the entry fee you pay to get in the door. What it actually buys you, further down the road, is something closer to quiet confidence, and you don't really notice how much of it you've gained until you catch yourself somewhere you wouldn't have predicted a year earlier. A few months ago I took my shirt off at a gym in a foreign country without a second thought. Twelve months before that, at the very start of this whole thing, that version of me genuinely couldn't have imagined it.

I'll be honest that it's tipped into its own kind of problem too, and I don't think I'd be telling this properly if I left that out. Calorie and protein tracking has rewired my brain in a way I didn't fully sign up for. It's borderline obsessive at this point, if I'm being straight about it, the running tally in my head before I've even sat down to eat. The Hevy app, where I log every workout, is part of the same thing. It makes the whole process feel competitive, mostly with myself, and I think that's a good version of it more often than not, but I'm aware it's a fine line between tracking something because it helps and tracking it because you can't stop.

There's a heavier side to this too, one I think about more than I used to. Corporate India is full of people running as hard as they possibly can to get ahead, skipping meals, stacking fourteen-hour days on top of each other for years. A lot of them only stop when the body forces the issue for them. A heart attack far too young. A kidney stone from three years of barely drinking water between back-to-back meetings. Some bill the body was quietly running up the whole time without ever sending a warning. Watching that happen to people I know, it's hard not to sit with the question of whether all of it was actually worth what it bought. Most people, once they've been through it, land on no. The frustrating part is how few of us reach that answer before the bill arrives instead of after.

A friend of mine says it better than I ever manage to. Your body is a temple, treat it like one. I used to think that was just a nice thing people say. I don't anymore. It's the plainest possible statement of the actual mechanism, because none of the other three work particularly well once this one quietly stops working.

## Friends and family, a close circle

Most months I split my time between Bengaluru and Noida, somewhere around seven to ten days with some of my closest friends from university in Bengaluru, and the rest back home, mostly because my client base is Delhi-heavy. What's stayed remarkably stable through all of that, even as everything around it has changed, is a small ritual on each side. Whenever I'm home on a free weekend, the same four of us from school still meet up, the way we have for years. It used to mean drinks and eating out somewhere loud. These days it's more likely to be the gym together, healthy food ordered in, board games or football playing in the background while we talk about nothing in particular. The exact same shape repeats itself in Bengaluru, just with the college crowd standing in where the school friends would be. I try not to take either version of it for granted. Being able to show up somewhere and have people genuinely want you there is a real privilege, and I only started to understand how real it is once I noticed, slowly, how many people around me don't have anything close to it.

The travel is the actual cost of it, and I don't think I've ever said that plainly before now. It's the biggest downside of living this split life, more than I let on most of the time. There's always a version of guilt sitting underneath it somewhere, being in Bengaluru and knowing I'm missing something small back home, or being in Noida and knowing the Bengaluru circle is out living a week of their lives without me in it. Nobody's doing anything wrong. It's just the price of choosing both instead of picking one, and some months it feels worth it and some months it just feels tiring.

Family runs on a slower, less forgiving clock than friendship does, and it took a piece of writing to actually make that land for me in a way conversations never quite had. There's an essay by Tim Urban called The Tail End, not Naval, as I'd assumed until I actually went back and checked, that does a piece of math on this more plainly than anything else I've come across. If you're twenty five and your parents are fifty five, and you see them twice a year, you might have somewhere around fifty visits left with them in total. Not fifty years. Fifty visits, total, ever. I remember sitting with that number for a while the first time I read it, and it genuinely changed how I think about the small decision to push a visit back another month because something at work felt urgent.

The honest shape of this, at least in my own life, looks more like a graph than a straight line. While you're deep in the grind of trying to get somewhere, the time with people you love thins out almost without you noticing, not because you love them any less but because there are only so many hours in a week and the grind tends to claim most of them first. Then, somewhere on the other side of that stretch, you find your way back to them, and you come back with more appreciation than you left with, not less. And at some point, for most people I know, there's a quieter realization sitting underneath all of it, that not everyone gets loving parents or siblings in the first place, that it's a privilege and not a given. Once that actually lands, it gets a lot harder to treat the people you do have as background noise you'll get to eventually.

## Partner

Someone about fifteen years older than me said something over LIITs in Pune a few years back that I still think about more than almost anything else anyone's told me. You're not finding a partner, he said. You're finding somebody to be the mother of your children. Written down like that it sounds almost cold, clinical even, but I think that's exactly what makes it useful. It strips away everything about the decision that's really about how someone looks in a photo, or how the relationship performs for an audience of people watching from the outside, and leaves you with the only question that actually matters underneath all of it. Whether this is someone who's still going to be there on your worst day, not just your best one.

The best advice I've been given about the rest of it is simpler than it sounds and somehow harder to actually live by. Find someone who's as genuinely invested in their own life and work as you are in yours. I've come to believe the healthiest version of this isn't two people so wrapped up in each other that neither one has any room left to do the things they individually love. It's two people walking their own paths in roughly the same direction, close enough to reach for each other whenever it matters, without either one losing the shape of who they were before the relationship started. Almost every version of this going badly that I've watched up close, in my own life or a friend's, seems to trace back to that same line getting crossed in one direction or the other. Either too little of two lives actually shared, or so much of both lives fused together that neither person can breathe on their own anymore.

I'm glad to say I have someone who genuinely fits that description, and I don't say that lightly. But I'd be lying if I said the logic just works because you've decided it should. It doesn't, not every time. Humans are emotional, not spreadsheets, and two people's schedules and moods and bad days rarely line up as neatly as the idea sounds when you say it out loud. I'm still learning how to actually live this out day to day rather than just believe in it, still figuring out where the line sits between giving someone space and just being absent, and somewhere in the middle of all that adjusting, it's still genuinely fun most of the time, which I think is the part that makes the rest of it worth getting right.

## Four clocks, not one checklist

I don't think these four things compound the same way, and that might be the closest thing to an actual lesson in all of this. Income seems to hit a ceiling on what it can buy pretty early, that 2x that barely moves anything is the proof of it, and past a certain point I think the marginal hour spent chasing more of it is close to wasted, not evil, just not worth what it costs. Health and friendship and family and partnership don't level off the same way at all. They seem to compound quietly for as long as you keep putting something into them, and they decay just as quietly, invisibly, right up until the moment they very much aren't invisible anymore. A health scare. A parent you didn't visit enough while you still could. A relationship that got either too tangled or never tangled enough to survive the years.

I don't think the actual skill here is trying to maximize all four of these at once, forever. I've never once seen anyone actually pull that off, and I suspect pretending it's possible is exactly how people end up rich and alone, thirty pounds past the point where a trek stops being something they can enjoy. The skill, if there is one, seems to be something closer to honesty. Noticing which of these four clocks is actually running low for you specifically, this year, this season of your life, and having the honesty to stop pouring more into the one that's already full.
