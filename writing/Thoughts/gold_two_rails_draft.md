# Gold Isn't Really Moving East

Hong Kong imported roughly 17 tonnes of Russian gold in 2022. The next year that number was 68 tonnes, four times over. By 2025 it was 92 tonnes, up 42 percent again. Seven months into 2026 it's already near 100, running at three times last year's pace. Plot those four numbers and you get a clean exponential curve, the kind that's genuinely rare in trade data and genuinely worth explaining.

The explanation making the rounds this week is tidy. For a century gold had three homes: London for trading, New York for official reserves, Dubai for physical flow into Asia. That arrangement is supposedly being redrawn into two rails, a Western one running through London and New York and a China-centred one running through Hong Kong, and the Hong Kong numbers are the proof.

Here's the actual claim of this piece. That framing takes two unrelated anxieties and one unrelated war, none of which have anything to do with each other, and sells them as a single migration because a single migration is a better story than three separate footnotes. Split them apart and each one gets easier to reason about. None of them, on its own, says what the combined version says.

## The first anxiety: a sanctions workaround with nowhere else to go

Start with why the Russian gold specifically goes to Hong Kong rather than mainland China, since that detail carries the whole first anxiety. Mainland China caps how much gold can be imported under quota. Hong Kong has no such cap. It also runs a currency pegged to the dollar, deep enough banking infrastructure to clear large bullion trades, and decades of prior trade relationships with Moscow that didn't need building from scratch. Gold headed for Chinese demand can sit in a Hong Kong vault and cross into the mainland whenever quota space opens, which makes Hong Kong less a rival capital of gold and more a waiting room next to one.

This is also, not coincidentally, exactly the kind of gap that gets a jurisdiction sanctioned. In 2024, the US Treasury designated three Hong Kong firms, Holden International Trading, Taube Precious HK, and VPower Finance Security, for laundering gold on behalf of Polyus, Russia's largest gold miner and itself already under US sanctions. The scheme routed payments through Hong Kong and UAE shell companies and converted them into cash and crypto to get the money back into Russia's financial system. The London Bullion Market Association pulled VPower's membership the same week. None of that stopped the flow. It confirmed what the flow actually was.

None of this means Hong Kong is only laundering someone else's sanctioned metal. On July 7, 2026, Hong Kong's Financial Services and Treasury Bureau began trialing a central gold clearing and settlement system, explicitly framed as a bid to beat Singapore to becoming Asia's primary bullion hub. That's genuine infrastructure, not a rumor, and it's the strongest evidence in favor of the two-rails story. But durable volume and legitimate volume aren't the same claim. It's entirely possible to build real plumbing on top of a flow that exists, in significant part, because Russian gold has nowhere else profitable to go. The infrastructure being real doesn't make the underlying demand clean. It makes the workaround more efficient. This anxiety is Moscow's, not Beijing's, and it would exist at the exact same size whether or not a single Western central bank ever moved an ounce.

## The second anxiety: one vault, not one hemisphere

The two-rails story needs Western central banks to be leaving the West at the same time Russian gold is arriving in Hong Kong, so that both moves point the same direction. Look at the actual banks and they don't.

France did complete a full withdrawal from the New York Fed in January 2026, 129 tonnes, booking a roughly €13 billion gain in the process. That part holds. The Netherlands is where the story breaks. The Dutch central bank also moved gold this year, 86 tonnes, but the direction was New York and Ottawa into London, not out of London. Before the move, London held about 18 percent of the Netherlands' reserves. After it, London holds 32 percent, the largest single share. That isn't a country stepping off the Western rail. It's a country consolidating deeper into one specific station on it while stepping away from a different one entirely.

Put France and the Netherlands side by side and what survives isn't "the West is de-risking away from the West." It's narrower: several central banks are quietly reducing exposure to one particular vault in Manhattan. Germany's Bundesbank still holds 1,236 tonnes there, over a third of its reserve, with domestic political pressure from multiple parties to bring more home. Poland has been adding to reserves held domestically. Serbia announced full domestic custody. The World Gold Council's 2025 survey found 59 percent of central banks now keep at least part of their gold on home soil, up from 41 percent the year before. That's real. What it isn't is a shift toward China. London is still very much inside it, arguably strengthening inside it. The thing actually losing share is a single building, not a hemisphere. This anxiety is about counterparty risk in one specific jurisdiction, and it would exist at the exact same size whether or not Hong Kong had ever imported a single bar from Moscow.

## The false third leg: a war, not a rail

Dubai completes the picture the two-rails story wants, and it's the part that holds up least. Gold shipments there did seize up for ten days in February and March 2026, when the Iran-Israel-US conflict closed UAE airspace and stranded bullion at $10 to $30 an ounce below the London benchmark. The World Gold Council's own strategists described it at the time as a regional disruption, not a structural one, pointing out that Singapore, Switzerland, and a large reserve of unallocated kilo bars sitting in New York could all absorb the slack. A hub that goes dark for ten days and gets covered by three backups elsewhere isn't evidence of a rail failing. It's evidence the old system still has redundancy the new one hasn't had to prove yet. A war closing an airport for ten days is weather. It isn't plumbing being rerouted.

## What's actually real underneath all three

Take the sanctions workaround, the vault-specific de-risking, and the war-driven disruption back out of the story, and one thing is still standing on its own. The marginal buyer of gold really has changed character over the last three years, from Western ETF flows that move with real yields and reverse fast, to central bank and household buying in China and Russia that moves with policy and doesn't reverse nearly as easily. That's a genuine structural shift in who sets the floor under the price. It's the one claim in the original story that doesn't need any of the other three to be true, and it's the only one of the four that will still be true next year regardless of how the other three resolve.

## India already made the vault move, two years early

Here's what the two-rails version leaves out entirely, and it isn't a footnote. India isn't a bystander watching France and the Netherlands rethink where they keep their gold. India ran its own version of the second anxiety first.

In May 2024, the RBI repatriated over 100 tonnes of gold from the Bank of England and the Bank for International Settlements, its first large-scale transfer back to Indian soil since 1991, when 47 tonnes went the other way as collateral during the balance-of-payments crisis. By September that year domestic holdings had risen from roughly 408 tonnes to 510, out of a total reserve of about 855 tonnes. Officials called it a logistics and storage-cost decision. It got a fraction of the attention France's withdrawal gets now. It's the identical move: a country deciding that gold sitting in someone else's vault becomes a liability the moment that someone else's politics turn unpredictable, and that storing it at home is worth the cost of removing the question entirely. If the second anxiety here is real, India isn't reacting to it. India is one of its earlier data points.

## The infrastructure India built before Hong Kong did, and the volume it still doesn't have

There's a second India angle, and it's less flattering. India built its own answer to Hong Kong's clearing system four years before Hong Kong built theirs. The India International Bullion Exchange opened at GIFT City in July 2022, backed by the NSE, BSE's India INX, CDSL, NSDL and MCX, pitched explicitly as a way to make India a price-setter in bullion rather than a price-taker, the same ambition Hong Kong is chasing against Singapore now.

IIBX exists, is regulated, and processes real trade. It never became what Hong Kong's system is already on track to become, and the reason has nothing to do with the exchange's design. Hong Kong's volume didn't show up because Hong Kong built good plumbing. It showed up because Russian gold had nowhere else profitable to go, and the plumbing got built to catch a flow that already existed under duress. IIBX has no equivalent forced flow behind it. It has ambition, regulation, and institutional backing, and none of that manufactures the one thing that actually fills an exchange: a class of gold locked out of every other route and needing somewhere to land. Good infrastructure attracts volume that has somewhere else to go if it wants to. It rarely creates volume that doesn't need to move at all.

## What this actually means if you run or fund a company

Two things follow, and neither one is really about whether you personally own gold.

The structural claim, the price-insensitive buyer raising the floor, is worth keeping in view for a reason that has nothing to do with bullion. It's a genuine data point on confidence in dollar-denominated assets broadly, and if your fund, your cap table, or your revenue is priced in dollars, that confidence question touches you whether or not you ever buy an ounce. Central banks quietly reducing exposure to one specific vault isn't a gold story wearing a disguise. It's an early, slow-moving read on how seriously large institutional actors are hedging against dollar-denominated risk, and that's the same instinct that eventually shows up in currency volatility, in how LPs think about denominating a fund, and in how a foreign acquirer prices a dollar-priced deal.

The more useful lesson is the method, not the metal. The exact move this piece just made, take a narrative where every individual fact checks out, and ask whether the facts actually belong to the same cause, is the same move worth making on the next pitch deck that says a category is inflecting, or the next board memo that cites de-dollarization or a rate outlook to justify urgency. A true, dramatic-looking chart doesn't arrive pre-attached to the right explanation in a fundraising narrative any more than it does in a gold thread. It gets attached to whichever explanation raises the round faster or justifies the valuation better, and the individual data points backing it are often completely real. The skill this piece is actually selling isn't a view on gold. It's the discipline of asking, before accepting any compressed story, whether the parts were ever actually one thing.

## The number was never the problem

The Hong Kong curve at the top of this piece is real, and it will keep climbing regardless of which of these stories turns out to be the one doing the explaining. That's exactly the danger in a number that clean. A true, dramatic-looking curve doesn't come pre-attached to the right explanation, in a gold thread or in a pitch deck. It gets attached to whichever explanation is most shareable that month, and a shareable explanation that's three-quarters right is more durable than a correct one that takes four paragraphs, because nobody fact-checks a trenchcoat. They just count the legs. The next one you're handed won't be about gold at all. It'll be a chart in a deck or a line in a board update, every number in it true, and the only question worth asking is the one this piece just asked about Hong Kong: do these facts actually belong to the same cause, or did someone just find a better trenchcoat.
