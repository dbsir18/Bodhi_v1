# Once Everyone Knows the Strategy

We had a banger day of Splendor recently, the kind of session that ends in an argument instead of a winner. Somewhere past midnight the strategy talk was still going. Guild play, taking the nobles early. Vertical play, building the engine up through the levels. Defensive play, reserving cards just to deny them. Everyone had a theory, and everyone had a game they could point to where the theory had won. Then somebody asked the question that shut the whole thing down, not because anyone answered it, but because nobody could. Once everybody at this table knows all of these strategies, how does the game actually play out?

I went looking for an answer and found something better, an old essay about game design that takes luck and skill apart properly. They are two separate measurements, not two ends of one scale. Poker is high on both. Chess is high skill and nearly luck-free. Tic-tac-toe is low on both, which is why nobody argues about how to play it. Luck, in this framework, is the uncertainty left in the outcome after you have accounted for every decision every player made. The game hands everyone the same shuffled deck, and whatever variance is still standing after skill has done its work, that is the game's luck.

*The same game can sit in two places. The table moves it.*

## The table sets the luck level

Our 3am conversation was circling a point none of us had the words for yet. A game's luck is not printed on the box. It depends on who sits down.

Skill decides games where skill differs. Between a newcomer and a veteran, Splendor is mostly a skill game, and the veteran wins without drama. My friends are the wrong group for that experiment. These are people who got into the colleges that admit you for ranking above the 99th percentile in exams lakhs of people sit for. When a table full of people like that learns its strategies from the same videos and the same forum threads, the skill gap between them compresses to a sliver, and what decides the outcome is mostly the variance. The exam that ranked us all had error bars too, one bad morning, one guessed answer, and we have each spent years being treated as a clean measurement anyway.

I have started to think that is exactly why game night with this group is so loud.

## The math of the middle

There is a way to say this with numbers, and the numbers are simple enough to be worth saying. Every result on game night is two things added together: the skill gap between the players, and noise, the leftover randomness of shuffles, draws, and dice. How much of a result was luck comes down to the size of the noise next to the size of the gap.

A game between a newcomer and a veteran is mostly gap. A game between four people who learned from the same videos is almost all noise, because the gap compressed to a sliver while the noise stayed the size of the game.

And there is a second consequence, the one that explains why the 3am argument is permanent. Suppose guild play is genuinely better, and suppose generously that it is worth two points a game. Suppose the noise of what comes off the deck is worth six points in either direction. To see a two-point edge through six points of fog you need repetition, and repetition works slowly: the fog only thins by the square root of the number of games played. Halve the fog, quadruple the games. Run the arithmetic with any honest guesses and settling guild versus vertical takes something on the order of a hundred games, played under conditions game night has never once had, same players, same seats. The argument never ends for an arithmetic reason, and I find that oddly comforting.

With those guesses, and one game's noise worth six points, the fog around the average result thins like this:

| Games played | Fog around the average result |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | ±6 points |
| 4 | ±3 points |
| 16 | ±1.5 points |
| 64 | ±0.75 points |
| 256 | ±0.375 points |

## Eight destinations, three points

A few weeks ago one of us completed eight destination tickets in Ticket to Ride and lost. Eight. Every strategy guide written about that game says destinations are the scoring engine. The other player spent his trains on the long routes instead, built the eight-length tunnel, collected longest road, and won by three points. Three. Smaller than the swing of one unlucky route draw. When the story got retold, the word used was "unreal", and it is the right word, because nothing about the result was fake. Two people played well, the margin landed inside the game's noise floor, and the scoreboard printed it as a victory for long routes.

## Turn one

The 3am conversation also left me with a question I have not put down since. How do you make sure the player going first doesn't always win? Designers answer this for a living, in rules nobody reads twice. Catan snakes its opening placement so the last player puts down two settlements back to back. Other games hand turn order to whoever is losing, which makes being behind a form of power. Every rule like this is a hand on the same dial. Whoever builds the game is choosing how much of the outcome to hand to variance.

## The experiment nobody has run yet

I have a small suite of rigged games I built for laughs, house-always-wins parodies, and so far nobody has played them. The plan for them was comedy. The luck conversation has given them a second job.

The experiment I want to run needs two tables and the same rigged game. One table is told the house is cheating. The other is told nothing. Then watch the first move. Does the table that knows hunt for the cheat instead of the win, and how fast does it stop playing the game in front of it and start playing the game behind it? Does the table that doesn't know blame itself, or the dice, or its own strategy? And does either table end up having less fun, because that prediction is the one I am least sure about and the one I care about most.

## Once everybody knows

I don't have a closing answer for the question that ended the night, and I have stopped wanting one. Once everybody knows the strategy, the game plays out loud and more or less on the draw. As it turns out, that is a banger of a way to spend a Sunday.
